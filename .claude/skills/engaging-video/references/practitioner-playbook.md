# What successful creators and strategists actually do

Attributed working methods, compiled November 2026. Read this when a user wants concrete examples, names a creator's style, or asks "how do the big channels do it".

**Sourcing caution.** Only the MrBeast memo passages were checked against a full text copy. Everything else comes from search summaries of interviews, posts and write-ups, so present these as "as reported" and never put words in quotation marks as a verified quote. These are also the habits of people who succeeded; they are not controlled tests, and they disagree with each other in places (see the last section).

## 1. The MrBeast production memo (internal onboarding document, leaked September 2024)

- **The goal is the best *YouTube* video**, explicitly not the best-produced, funniest or best-looking one.
- **Three numbers:** click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed. Plus an untrackable **"wow factor"**: something no other creator would do. The example given is bringing a contestant's house in by crane thirty seconds into a video, which added nothing to the title or thumbnail.
- **Idea leverage:** the same effort with a different premise ("50 hours in my front yard" versus "in ketchup") is described as easily a hundred times more viral.
- **Packaging sets expectations.** If the thumbnail shows a giant yellow castle beside skyscrapers and the video shows a red one in a field, the viewer feels lied to and leaves. A thumbnail sketch exists before production starts.
- **Every stretch of the runtime has a job:**
  - *First minute:* the most important, with the largest loss. Failures listed: poor lighting, not matching the packaging, unplanned wording, not front-loading the interesting material.
  - *Minutes 1–3:* move from hype to execution; stop telling viewers what they will see and show them. "Crazy progression": cover a lot of ground fast so the viewer is already invested.
  - *Around minute 3:* a "re-engagement", something impressive that fits the story, placed where boredom is expected.
  - *Minutes 3–6:* exciting but simple content that makes viewers care about the story and the people.
  - *Around minute 6:* a second re-engagement that pushes into the back half.
  - *Back half:* viewers are in a lull and tend to finish, but a bad back half has killed videos.
  - *Ending:* never signal the end unless it builds anticipation for the payoff.
- **Formats that hold attention:** payoff-at-the-end structures ("last to leave"), and "stair stepping", where each step is cooler than the last.
- **Also:** no dull moments; show the work to as many people as possible before publishing; integrate sponsors into the content because retention dips less than with a separate ad read.

These timings were tuned for 15-minute-plus challenge videos. For other lengths, treat them as proportions: front-load, switch to showing early, put a high point about a quarter and half-way in, hide the ending.

**His own revision (public post, March 2024):** he slowed the videos down, focused on storytelling, let scenes breathe and yelled less, said views rose, and told creators to drop the ultra-fast overstimulating style because it doesn't work. A trade-press count found 38 cuts in the first minute of a 2023 video against 23 in a 2024 one.

## 2. Idea and packaging: the strategists

**Paddy Galloway** (strategist for large channels)
- Don't spend 5% of your time on what is 50% of the result. His clients reportedly make 10 or more thumbnails per video, sometimes 50.
- "The algorithm is the audience": think about viewers, not the system.
- Most of a channel's growth comes from one idea that becomes a repeatable format; be an idea machine. A reported funnel: 100 ideas → 10 → 1.
- Thumbnails: three or fewer major elements; the **glance test** (can everything be processed in the instant a viewer scrolls past?); judge title and thumbnail as one unit that complements rather than repeats.
- Borrow proven formats from unrelated niches. Study videos that beat their own channel's baseline several times over.

**Jake Thomas** (Creator Hooks)
- Three click-worthy emotions: curiosity, fear, desire. The best titles pair curiosity with one of the others. In about a thousand titles he analysed, 60% used curiosity, 46% desire, 39% fear.
- Build curiosity by knowing what the audience assumes and challenging it.
- Give the title and thumbnail different jobs; repeating the title in the thumbnail wastes it.
- Model what has already worked in your niche or a neighbouring one.

**Ed Lawrence** (Film Booth)
- About 70% of the work is planning: settle the title and thumbnail until he believes people will click, then make the video, then write the intro.
- Borrow a proven title and adjust it slightly.
- Script an intro that connects the packaging to the opening.

**Derral Eves** (*The YouTube Formula*)
- Audience before algorithm. Try → fail → analyse → adjust. Shoot thumbnail images before recording.

## 3. Hooks and scripting

**Aprilynne Alter**
- In the first five seconds, the opening must match what the title and thumbnail promised, or viewers get confused. Two promises to meet immediately: a **plot promise** (what happens) and a **format promise** (what kind of video this is).
- Her study of MrBeast openings: the first sentence matches the title, the first shot matches the thumbnail, and effort invested is shown within twenty seconds.
- A thumbnail far more polished than the video undermines trust.

**George Blackman** (scriptwriter)
- **Setup → tension → payoff** is a loop per segment, repeated roughly every one to two minutes, and the tool he uses to diagnose retention graphs.
- Write payoffs first, then setups, then the hook, then the tension.
- Hook formula: *Target, Transformation, Stakes* for educational videos; *Character, Concept, Stakes* for entertainment.
- The common failure is paying off instantly, leaving no tension.
- Before scripting, find two videos with packaging like yours, one that did well and one that didn't, and look for the difference.
- Signpost: remind viewers where they are at each new segment.

## 4. Story and editing: long-form creators

**Ryan Trahan and editor Zach Levet** (penny series)
- Restate the mission constantly; without the goal, the series is just a vlog.
- A recurring "game plan" segment each episode gives context for what's about to happen.
- Build in jeopardy: a rule that can wipe out progress.

**Mark Rober**
- "Hide the vegetables": a spectacular wrapper draws people in, and the substance rides inside it.
- Focus on the feeling; be authentic. Make 50 videos without expecting anyone to watch.

**Veritasium (Derek Muller)**
- Title and thumbnail largely decide a video's success. He separates honest intrigue from deception, and swaps titles and thumbnails after publishing to keep the better performer.

**Hayden Hillier-Smith** (editor)
- Show the story, don't tell it: how little narration can the edit get away with?
- Cutting to b-roll rests the viewer's attention while the audio continues.
- Showing an image slightly too briefly creates curiosity.
- Short recurring music cues set tone quickly. He describes himself as focused on emotion, not on retention.

**Casey Neistat**
- Three-act structure even in spontaneous vlogs; how the story is told matters more than the subject; deliberate imperfection keeps it human.

**Dhar Mann** (per YouTube's own write-up)
- Hook within seconds, a clear and instantly familiar conflict, build to a climax, check the retention graph for drop-offs.

**Ali Abdaal**
- Reported that a thumbnail change through testing took one video from 300,000 to 1.1 million views.

## 5. Short-form

**Jenny Hoyos** (Shorts)
- Decide the **last line before filming**, so the final beat is known.
- Hook in about a second, then **foreshadow in two lines, three seconds or less**.
- Replace "and" with **"but" and "so"** so every line creates or resolves conflict.
- Cut any line that breaks pace, including throat-clearing like "let's get started".
- The payoff lands in the final second; ask whether a viewer would rewatch.
- A weak hook is her first suspect when a Short fails.
- About 20 hours per Short. She also says retention is over-weighted: some Shorts with excellent retention stall.

**Alex Hormozi**
- Hook (stop the scroll) → retain → reward (pay off the hook).
- No such thing as too long, only too boring.
- His operation reportedly cuts clips from the moments where the long-form retention graph spikes, since those are already proven to hold attention.

**Gary Vaynerchuk**
- One long "pillar" piece cut into many "micro" pieces across platforms. "Document, don't create" as an antidote to perfectionism.

## 6. Gaming and devlogs

This is the thinnest area: no first-hand packaging advice from major gaming creators was retrievable. What exists is general and mostly from blogs and forums.

- Title with the most interesting moment or outcome, never "Devlog #14 — AI update". Specific beats clever ("I beat X without taking damage").
- Decide why anyone should watch, then break the story into chapters around a problem and how it was solved. Avoid feature bullet lists.
- Before/after thumbnails make progress readable at a glance.
- The MrBeast memo describes its core audience as game-playing teenagers, so its structure was tuned on a gaming-adjacent audience.
- If the aim is to sell a game: game-marketing data (Chris Zukowski) suggests viral social moments convert to very few wishlists, and that devlogs mostly reach other developers. Track views and wishlists as separate goals.

## 7. Testing and reading results

- **YouTube's Test & Compare** tests up to three titles and/or thumbnails and picks by watch time share. If there's no clear winner the first option stays.
- Practitioners test at least three thumbnail concepts at launch and read early click-through.
- **A good click-through rate is one better than your last video** (YouTube's creator liaison).
- If a video does badly with existing subscribers, look at the topic and title first (YouTube staff).
- Map each retention dip to the script segment that caused it.

## 8. Cadence and time to traction

- MrBeast: make 100 videos, improving one thing each time; one excellent video beats a hundred mediocre ones.
- Mark Rober: 50 videos before expecting anything.
- Galloway (as summarised): expect twelve months and 50-plus videos to find your groove.
- YouTube staff: upload frequency is neither rewarded nor punished in itself.
- Large vendor datasets show channels that upload more often grow faster, while their authors say this doesn't prove frequency causes growth.

A workable reading: pick the highest pace at which every video still gets a real idea filter, three packaging options and one named improvement.

## 9. Advice that is now outdated

| Old advice | Current position |
|---|---|
| Cut faster, be louder | Retracted by MrBeast himself in 2024 |
| Maximise click-through | YouTube's own test picks by watch time |
| Tags drive discovery | Minimal impact, per YouTube's liaison |
| The algorithm rewards a posting schedule | Viewer behaviour matters, not the schedule |
| The first hour or day decides a video's fate | Denied by YouTube staff; many videos earn most views a month or more later |
| Put the title text in the thumbnail | Wasted space, per Thomas, Galloway and Alter |
| Retention percentage is everything | Hoyos and Hillier-Smith both say it's over-weighted; the memo itself says a video can hit the numbers and still underperform |

## 10. Where practitioners disagree

Present these as choices, not settled answers:

- **Retention graph as scoreboard** (the MrBeast memo) versus **emotion over metrics** (Hillier-Smith, Hoyos's caveat). MrBeast's 2024 shift moved toward the second view.
- **A few heavily engineered pieces** (Hoyos, 20 hours per Short) versus **volume through repurposing** (Hormozi, Vaynerchuk).
- **Hold back the best material** so there's a reason to stay (Lawrence) versus **open with the most explosive points** and then connect them (Blackman).
- **One second** versus **three seconds** to hook on Shorts.
