# Why people click, watch, finish and share

What the research supports, how strongly, and what it means when scripting and editing. Compiled November 2026. Many source details were taken from search summaries rather than full-text reading, so treat exact figures as approximate and don't quote effect sizes to a user as settled.

Evidence labels: **Strong** = meta-analysis or independently replicated. **Moderate** = peer-reviewed but a single study, or from a different medium (ads, news headlines, online courses), so applying it to creator video is an inference. **Contested** = findings conflict or failed replication. **Industry** = vendor data on already-successful videos, not science. **Craft** = practitioner advice with no formal test.

## 1. Curiosity and information gaps

- **Curiosity is a gap between what someone knows and what they want to know** (Loewenstein, 1994). A little information increases the appetite; it fades once enough is known. You can only be curious about something you partly understand. *Strong as theory.*
- **It follows an inverted U.** People are least curious when they know nothing or feel sure of the answer, and most curious in the middle (Kang et al., 2009, trivia questions; small sample). *Moderate.*
- **The same shape appears in 27,616 real headline experiments** (Aubin Le Quere & Matias, 2025): vague headlines gain clicks from more concreteness, very concrete ones lose from it. *Strong for headlines; inference for video titles.*
- **Create-then-resolve beats telling everything at once** (the "teasing effect", Ruan, Hsee & Lu, 2018): people enjoy an experience more when uncertainty is raised and then resolved, even though they say they'd prefer everything up front. *Moderate.*
- **Curiosity pulls regardless of payoff quality** (Hsee & Ruan, 2016): people chose unpleasant outcomes just to resolve uncertainty. This is why empty clickbait gets clicks, and why the click alone proves nothing about the video. *Moderate.*
- **People tend to resume what they've started** (the Ovsiankina effect held up in a 2025 meta-analysis). *Supported.*
- **Surprise makes the payoff stick.** Curiosity improved memory specifically for surprising answers; reward systems respond to the unexpected and barely at all to the fully predicted. *Moderate.*

**Use it:** Give enough concrete context that the viewer can name the specific thing they don't know. Test a title with two questions: can the viewer state the question the video answers (if not, add concreteness), and can they state the answer (if so, remove it but keep the stakes). Open the main question in the first seconds and close it at the end; open and close smaller ones along the way. Make the first shot visibly continue what was clicked on. Aim for a reveal slightly different from what the viewer guessed.

## 2. Emotion: clicking, staying, sharing, commenting

These four are driven by different emotional profiles. Most studies measure only one, so the mapping across them is a synthesis.

- **Sharing is driven by arousal, not just positivity** (Berger & Milkman, 2012). High-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, excitement, anger, anxiety) increase sharing; low-arousal ones (sadness, contentment) do not. *Strong.*
- **On YouTube specifically** (Tellis et al., 2019, video ads): inspiration, excitement, amusement and warmth raised sharing; plot helped; information-heavy content was shared *less*; early or prominent branding reduced sharing. *Strong.*
- **Holding attention:** surprise concentrates attention; joy keeps people from leaving, and the *change* in joy matters more than its level, so several separate bursts beat one sustained stretch (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters, 2012; small lab study of ads). *Moderate.*
- **Clicking:** mildly negative wording lifts headline click-through by a couple of percent, with sadness words working better than anger or fear (Upworthy archive, 105,000+ headlines). Small effect, and the opposite of the sharing pattern. *Strong for news headlines.*
- **Commenting and reacting:** moral-emotional and us-versus-them language reliably increases shares and angry reactions on political text posts (Brady et al., 2017; Rathje et al., 2021). The authors call it a perverse incentive, and outrage framing lowered credibility in headline experiments. *Strong for the effect; the trust cost is moderate.*

**Use it:** Mark the intended emotion on each beat. Put an early surprise, then spaced peaks of humour, small wins or reveals. Build at least one awe or "how is that possible" moment that stands alone as a clip. Calm or sad content can be valued but won't spread by sharing unless it ends on something activating. Don't front-load sponsor mentions. Leave outrage bait alone.

## 3. Story

- **Being absorbed in a story changes attitudes and intentions**, and the effect is stronger for creator-made content than for professional content (van Laer et al., meta-analyses 2014 and 2019). *Strong.*
- **Complete dramatic structure travels further.** In 155 viral video ads, those with four or five acts averaged more than four times the shares of those with three or fewer (Quesenberry & Coolsen, 2019). Correlational, and sampled from already-viral ads. *Moderate.*
- **"But / therefore", not "and then"** (Trey Parker and Matt Stone): each beat should connect to the next by conflict or consequence. *Craft*, but it enforces the causal coherence the research says stories need.
- **Looking into the camera makes viewers feel addressed; it does not by itself make them identify with you.** Identification needs a goal and a vulnerability the viewer shares. *Moderate.*

**Use it:** Even an informational video benefits from a spine: someone wants something, an obstacle appears, there's a consequence, a turn, a resolution. State the stake early and concretely. Finish the story. Tell it as your own experience.

## 4. Attention and cognitive load

- **There is no fixed attention span.** Attention depends on the task and on whether the content keeps earning it.
- **Cut the extraneous, signal the essential, segment the material** (Mayer's multimedia principles). Robust for learning outcomes, though independent meta-analyses find smaller effects than Mayer's own lab. *Strong.*
- **In 6.9 million online-course viewing sessions** (Guo, Kim & Rubin, 2014): short segments held attention best, and informal, enthusiastic, fairly fast delivery beat polished studio footage. *Strong for educational video.*
- **More edits within a scene raised arousal and memory** in television-era lab work (Lang et al., 2000), via the orienting response to novelty. *Moderate, old, different medium.* Nothing supports any specific cut interval.
- **Sound-off viewing:** the widely quoted 85% and 92% figures are 2016–2019 Facebook-era publisher and survey numbers. No current measurement for TikTok, Reels or Shorts was found; those feeds default to sound on. About half of Americans in a 2022 survey said they usually watch with subtitles. *Industry, weak.*
- **The opening is where the audience is lost.** YouTube Studio reports the share still watching at 30 seconds as a headline number, and compares retention to videos of similar length.

**Use it:** Remove what doesn't serve the title's promise. Put two to five key words on screen, not full sentences duplicating speech. Build in marked chunks with a small payoff each. Use visual change to mark a new idea and let pace vary. Caption short-form. Compare retention against your own videos, not anyone's published percentage.

## 5. Why viewers return to a person

- **One-sided bonds with on-screen people are robust** (meta-analysis of 120 studies, Tukachinsky et al., 2020) and are *not* explained by loneliness. *Strong.*
- **Direct address experimentally increases the feeling of interaction** (Hartmann & Goldhoorn, 2011; TV presenters). *Moderate.*
- **Self-disclosure and perceived similarity are associated with stronger bonds** in YouTuber surveys. *Weak to moderate, correlational.*
- **Familiarity builds liking up to a point, then turns down** (mere exposure, meta-analysis of 81 articles). *Strong.*

**Use it:** Look into the lens and talk to one viewer, especially at the start and at emotional beats. Include small, relevant, real admissions. Keep a recognisable format, voice and look, and vary what happens inside it: same show, new episode. Authenticity in practice means natural delivery, visible imperfection, and doing what the title said.

## 6. Endings, social proof and loss framing

- **The most intense moment and the final moment dominate how an experience is remembered** (peak-end effect, meta-analysis of 174 effects, large). But average quality predicts evaluations about as well. *Strong; applying it to video endings is by analogy.*
- **Visible popularity changes what people choose and makes success partly luck** (Salganik, Dodds & Watts, 2006). *Strong.*
- **YouTube measures satisfaction after viewing with surveys** and trains its system on the answers, so how a video is remembered feeds recommendations.
- **Loss aversion is real in many settings but its universality is disputed**; don't state the "twice as much" multiplier as a law. *Contested.*

**Use it:** End at the strongest payoff with no trailing outro. Build one clear high point and protect it. Real numbers and real reactions help. Loss framing can lift clicks slightly, when the loss is real and the video addresses it. Judge a format over many videos.

## 7. Titles and thumbnails

There is **no peer-reviewed causal evidence** linking thumbnail features to click-through. Everything is vendor description of winners, and the datasets disagree.

- Faces: common on breakout thumbnails (69–84% depending on the study) but nearly as common on underperformers (82%). *Industry.*
- Exaggerated expressions: about 5% of 500 breakout thumbnails (vidIQ, 2026). *Industry.*
- Text: about half of breakout thumbnails had three words or fewer; many had none. *Industry.*
- Titles: around six words or fewer is the most consistent pattern. *Industry, second-hand.*
- YouTube's Test & Compare chooses winners by **watch time share**, not clicks.

**Use it:** One idea per thumbnail, legible at phone size, little or no text, something that really appears in the video. Test genuinely different concepts and log results per channel, because niche effects swamp cross-channel averages.

## 8. Short-form feeds

- In a swipe feed nothing was chosen, so the opening frames do the work of thumbnail, title and intro at once, and staying versus swiping is the first thing the system learns. *Inference from platform mechanics.*
- YouTube reports "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts but publishes no benchmark; third-party targets conflict. TikTok's own ad guidance says to land the hook in the first three to six seconds.
- No peer-reviewed study measures how quickly viewers decide to swipe. The "one to three seconds" window is platform advice and creator consensus.
- Whether rewatches are a ranking signal appears only in third-party guides.

## 9. Popular claims that are wrong

| Claim | Reality |
|---|---|
| "Attention spans are 8 seconds, shorter than a goldfish" | Fabricated. Traces to a graphic in a 2015 marketing report sourced to a commercial statistics site. No evidence attention spans are shrinking. |
| "Open loops work because of the Zeigarnik effect" | A 2025 meta-analysis found no overall memory advantage for unfinished tasks. Use information-gap curiosity and the teasing effect instead. |
| "Each cut / reveal is a dopamine hit" | Reward prediction error is real and supports surprise and variety. Dopamine is not "the pleasure chemical" and the addiction framing isn't supported. |
| "Put a shocked face on every thumbnail" | Exaggerated expressions are rare among breakout videos; faces appear equally on winners and losers. |
| "Cut every N seconds" | No research supports any interval. |
| "People hate losing twice as much as they like winning" | Disputed as a general law. |
| "85% of video is watched muted" | A 2016 Facebook-era figure. No current short-form measurement found. |
| "Clickbait always backfires" | Not established either. It can raise clicks in the short run; the cost shows up in early exits, satisfaction and trust. |

## 10. Where the evidence runs out

Be candid about these when a user asks for certainty:

- No controlled study tests curiosity gaps in YouTube titles or thumbnails specifically.
- No study tests cut rate or "pattern interrupts" on YouTube or TikTok retention.
- No study tests "but/therefore" structure against "and then" on retention.
- No long-term study shows clickbait reducing return viewing for creators; that rests on headline-credibility experiments plus platform design.
- No direct evidence on habit or variable reward as reasons viewers return to a specific creator.
- The comparison of emotional drivers across clicking, watching, sharing and commenting is assembled from separate studies.
