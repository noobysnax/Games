# Engaging video

A method for making videos people choose, finish and pass on. It combines research on attention, curiosity, emotion and story with the documented working practices of successful creators and YouTube's own statements about what its systems measure.

## The one idea underneath everything

**An engaging video makes a specific promise and keeps it.** The title and thumbnail make the promise, the opening confirms it, the body keeps opening and closing smaller questions on the way to it, and the ending delivers it at full strength. Almost every technique below is a way of doing one of those four things, and almost every failure is a break in that chain: a vague promise, an opening that doesn't match the click, a middle with no reason to keep watching, or a payoff that is weaker than what was promised.

This matters mechanically as well as ethically. YouTube picks title/thumbnail test winners by watch time rather than clicks, and measures satisfaction with viewer surveys, so over-promising loses twice: the viewer leaves early, and the system learns the video disappoints.

## Work in this order

Creators and strategists who disagree on plenty agree on the order of operations, because each stage constrains the next:

1. **Idea** — sets the ceiling. No edit rescues an idea nobody wants.
2. **Packaging** (title + thumbnail) — written *before* scripting, because it defines the promise the video must keep.
3. **Opening** — confirms the click.
4. **Body** — a chain of setup → tension → payoff loops.
5. **Ending** — the strongest payoff, then stop.
6. **Edit pass** — remove everything that doesn't serve the promise.
7. **Audit** — check the promise was kept.
8. **Publish and learn** — one named improvement per video.

Start at whatever stage the user is at, but look one stage back: a weak hook is often a packaging problem, and weak packaging is often an idea problem. Say so when you see it rather than polishing the wrong layer.

## Before you start

Find out, or assume and state: the topic, who it is for, long-form or short-form, target platform and rough length, whether the creator appears on camera or narrates over footage, and what already exists (footage, script, a published video with analytics). Don't interrogate the user; if something is missing, pick a sensible assumption, say what you assumed, and keep going.

**Assume choices, never facts.** Format, length and audience are fair to assume. The creator's own story is not: how long something took, what went wrong, how many attempts, what result they got, what they felt. A hook built on an invented number is a false promise the moment it's published. Where the script needs one of these, write a bracketed placeholder (`[N weekends]`, `[the bug that cost you a day]`) and collect the placeholders in a short list of things the user needs to supply. If project files are available, read them for real details before reaching for a placeholder.

Read the reference files as needed:

- `references/practitioner-playbook.md` — attributed methods from named creators and strategists, with their disagreements, plus a section on gaming and devlog videos. Read when the user wants examples, names a creator's style, or is making a gameplay or devlog video.

## Stage 1 — Idea

Generate many, keep few. Aim for 20+ candidate ideas, filter to a handful, develop one. Judge each by asking:

- **Would the intended viewer click this with no context?** Compare the same effort with a different premise: "I spent 50 hours in my front yard" versus "in ketchup" is the same production and a completely different click.
- **Can the promise be stated in one concrete sentence?** If not, the idea isn't finished.
- **Is there proof of demand?** Look for videos on similar ideas that far outperformed their own channel's normal numbers (outliers), in this niche or an adjacent one. Model the premise or format, never the content itself. If you can search, compare a candidate video's views with that channel's typical views. If you can't, say demand is unchecked and give the user the two-minute check to run themselves: search the idea on YouTube, open a few results from small channels, and see whether any video has several times that channel's usual views.
- **What is the one thing here a viewer can't get from a similar channel?** It doesn't need budget; it needs to be distinctive.
- **Does it have built-in stakes?** Something can be won, lost, proven, broken or finished.

## Stage 2 — Packaging

Write the title and thumbnail concept as one unit, before the script.

**Titles.** Write at least 10 options and pick deliberately.

- Open exactly one curiosity gap. The viewer should be able to state the *question* the video answers but not the *answer*. Too vague ("You won't believe this") and too complete ("X is 12% faster than Y") both underperform; curiosity peaks in the middle.
- Pair curiosity with a second pull: something the viewer wants, or something they'd like to avoid.
- Concrete beats clever. Lead with the specific subject or outcome. Around six words or fewer is the most consistent pattern in industry data (correlational).
- Stronger wording raises clicks only up to what the video really delivers.
- Every title on the shortlist must make the *same* promise the whole video pays off, so that any of them fits the same opening line. A title that promises one early moment is a clip title, not a video title; move it to the clip candidates.

**Thumbnails.** Describe 3 genuinely different concepts, not colour variations.

- One idea, three or fewer major elements, readable in a glance at phone size.
- Zero to three words of text, and the text should add to the title, never repeat it.
- Show something that actually appears or happens in the video.
- A face is a reasonable default for personality-led channels, not a rule. Exaggerated shocked faces are rare among breakout videos in current data.

Recommend testing 2–3 variants with YouTube's Test & Compare and explain that the winner is chosen on watch time share.

## Stage 3 — Opening

The first 30 seconds is the steepest drop in every retention curve, so it gets the most care per second.

- **Confirm the click within about five seconds.** The first line should restate the title's promise and the first shot should match the thumbnail. Viewers who can't tell they got what they clicked for leave.
- **State the stake.** What is at risk, what will be different by the end, why it matters now.
- **Build the hook from three parts:** for teaching content, *who it's for → what changes for them → what's at stake*; for entertainment, *who → the concept → what's at stake*.
- **Show effort or proof early** if it exists (the 40 attempts, the six months, the result on screen).
- **Then start.** No greeting, no channel ident, no recap of what the title already said, no "before we begin".
- Move from telling to showing quickly. After the hook, stop describing what the viewer will see and show it.

## Stage 4 — Body

Structure the body as a series of loops, each one **setup → tension → payoff**, roughly every one to two minutes in long-form. Writing the payoffs first, then the setups, makes this easier.

- **Read the beat list aloud. Every "and then" between beats is a likely drop-off.** Rewrite each transition as "but" (a complication) or "therefore" (a consequence), so cause and conflict carry the viewer forward.
- **Don't pay off instantly.** A question answered in the same breath creates no tension. Hold it only as long as the story needs, though; delay that serves tension is craft, padding is not.
- **Escalate.** Each segment should be a step up from the last, with the best material arriving at the end.
- **Place a deliberate high point where boredom is expected** — about a quarter and about halfway through a long video. Something genuinely impressive that belongs to the story.
- **Restate the goal and where we are** at each new segment. Viewers who lose the thread leave.
- **Vary the emotional line.** Attention is held by change: an early surprise, then separate peaks of humour, small wins or reveals. A pleasant but flat stretch loses people.
- **Speak to one person, into the lens,** and include small real admissions (what went wrong, what you were unsure of). This is what builds the feeling of knowing the creator, which is why viewers come back. For narration over gameplay or screen footage, the equivalent is first-person voice ("I", "you"), a natural unscripted-sounding read, and showing the admissions on screen: the failed attempt, the ugly first version, the bug.

## Stage 5 — Ending

- End on, or immediately after, the strongest payoff. The last moment and the best moment weigh most heavily in how the whole video is remembered.
- Never signal that the video is ending unless it builds anticipation for the payoff. "So that's it for today" is an exit cue.
- No long outro, recap or housekeeping block. Keep any call to action to a sentence, placed just before the final payoff or folded into it.
- A strong ending cannot rescue a weak middle; average quality matters about as much as the peak and the end.

## Stage 6 — Edit pass

- **Cut anything that doesn't serve the promise**: tangents, decorative b-roll fighting the narration, dead air, repeated points. This is the best-supported edit rule there is.
- **Signal what matters**: put only the few key words on screen, zoom or highlight the relevant part of the frame, use spoken signposts.
- **Use visual change to mark a new idea** (cut, angle, graphic, location). Let the pace vary. There is no evidence for a magic cut interval, and constant fast cutting wears off just as a static shot does; MrBeast publicly abandoned the hyper-fast style in 2024.
- **Let important moments breathe.** Give the peak room.
- **Caption short-form** and make the first seconds understandable from picture and text alone.
- **Keep sponsor mentions out of the opening** and away from the peak.

## Stage 7 — Promise–payoff audit

Run this before anything is called finished:

1. Write the literal promise made by the title plus thumbnail.
2. Find the timestamp where the video delivers it. If there isn't one, change the packaging or the video.
3. Is the payoff at least as good as a reasonable viewer would expect? If the likely reaction is "that's it?", it's clickbait even if technically true.
4. Does the thumbnail image actually appear or happen?
5. Is any urgency, danger, scarcity or conflict in the framing real?
6. Is the answer withheld longer than the story needs?

Don't help manufacture outrage, fake stakes, or us-versus-them hostility for reactions. Those do produce shares and comments, and they are the engagement levers most clearly tied to lost trust. If the user asks for a title or hook the video can't back up, say so and offer the strongest honest version.

## Stage 8 — After publishing

- **Compare against the channel's own previous videos**, not third-party benchmarks, which conflict with each other. A good click-through rate is one better than last time.
- **Diagnose by pattern:**
  - Low clicks, good retention → the video works, the packaging doesn't. Test new titles/thumbnails.
  - High clicks, steep early drop → the opening doesn't match the promise. Fix the first 30 seconds or tone down the packaging.
  - Gradual sag in the middle → loops without tension, or "and then" structure. Map each dip to the script segment that caused it.
  - Cliff near the end → the ending was signalled, or the payoff came too early.
  - Spikes → rewatched moments. These are the best candidates for clips and for next video's idea.
- **Split subscribers from new viewers.** If existing subscribers ignore it, suspect the topic and title before blaming distribution.
- **Name one thing to improve next time** and write it down. The practitioners who give a number say 50–100 videos of this before expecting results.
- Don't judge a video in its first hour or day; YouTube staff say many videos earn most of their views weeks later. Judge a format over several videos, because outcomes are partly luck.

## What to produce

Match the output to the request; don't dump every stage on someone who asked for a title. Lead with the deliverable they asked for, then add the one or two upstream issues that matter most.

**For a new video plan**, use this structure:

```
# [Working title]
**Promise:** one sentence — what the viewer gets
**Viewer and format:** who, platform, target length, on camera or voiceover
**Assumptions:** what you assumed because the user didn't say
**Angle:** the idea you chose, why, and the two or three runners-up (don't list all 20)
**Demand check:** what you found, or "unchecked" plus how to check

## Packaging
Titles (10, best 3 marked, with why)
Thumbnail concepts (3, each: elements, text if any, what it shows from the video)

## Opening (first 30 seconds)
Word-for-word script, with the first shot described

## Beat outline
Numbered beats, each with: setup / tension / payoff, the but-or-therefore link to the next beat, the intended emotion
High points marked; ending beat marked

## Promise–payoff audit
The six checks, answered

## Clip candidates (if long-form)
3–5 moments that stand alone, each with its own hook line

## Needed from you
Every bracketed placeholder in the script, as a list of facts to supply
Shots or recordings still to capture, if the footage doesn't cover the outline
```

**For a review of a script, outline or cut**, give: a one-paragraph verdict; the biggest problem first with the stage it belongs to; specific rewrites (show the new line, don't just describe it); what is already working and should be kept.

**For a diagnosis of a published video**, give: the pattern you see, the most likely cause, the fix for this video if one exists (usually packaging), and the single change to carry into the next one.

## Being straight with the user

- **Never promise virality.** Outcomes are partly luck even for excellent videos, and most channels take over a year and dozens of uploads to reach 1,000 subscribers. Technique raises the odds; say that.
- **Separate what is proven from what is craft.** The references label evidence strength. Research supports the mechanisms (curiosity gaps, emotional arousal and sharing, story structure, cutting the extraneous, direct address, strong endings); most specific numbers creators quote (seconds, percentages, cut rates) are habit or vendor data. Present those as starting points to test.
- **Don't repeat debunked lore.** No 8-second attention span, no "dopamine hits", no Zeigarnik effect as proof, no mandatory shocked face, no magic cut interval, no tags-as-ranking-lever, no "first hour decides everything". `references/psychology.md` has the corrections.
- **Platform facts go stale.** Anything about limits, thresholds or policies in these files is dated late 2026 and came largely from secondary reporting. Check the platform's own help pages before the user relies on a specific number.
