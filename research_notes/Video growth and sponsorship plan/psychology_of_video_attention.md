# Psychology of Video Attention, Retention, and Sharing (long-form YouTube + short-form vertical)

Research date: 2026-11-20. Evidence labels used throughout:

- **[STRONG]** = meta-analytic or independently replicated peer-reviewed finding
- **[MODERATE]** = peer-reviewed, but single study / narrow sample / different medium (ads, news headlines, MOOCs) so transfer to creator video is an inference
- **[CONTESTED]** = peer-reviewed findings conflict, or the effect failed replication
- **[INDUSTRY]** = vendor / creator-tool data (vidIQ, 1of10, Wistia, Kapwing, Retention Rabbit etc.). Descriptive, non-random samples, usually survivorship-biased, often a marketing asset. Not science.
- **[PLATFORM]** = official statement from YouTube / TikTok (authoritative about what the platform measures, not about psychology)
- **[LORE]** = widely repeated creator claim with no good source

Method caveat for the report writer: direct page fetches were blocked in this session for pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, support.google.com, vidiq.com and 1of10.com, so details for those sources come from search-result extracts, not full-text reading. Numbers that could not be checked against the primary document are flagged "unverified against primary".

## 1. Curiosity and information gaps (hooks, titles, thumbnails; how much to reveal vs withhold)

### Takeaway
Curiosity is best supported as an inverted-U: it peaks when the viewer knows *something* and can see a specific, closable gap, and it is low both when they know nothing (too vague) and when they already feel they know the answer (too revealing). The Zeigarnik "open loops stick in memory" claim that creator lore leans on has largely failed replication; what holds up is the weaker, more useful claim that people want to *resume/finish* something they have started, and that creating and then resolving uncertainty makes the experience more enjoyable.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG as theory, well-cited]** Loewenstein (1994) frames curiosity as a feeling of deprivation that arises when attention is drawn to a gap between what one knows and what one wants to know; a small amount of information increases the appetite, and curiosity fades once enough is known. A noted limit: the theory implies you can only be curious about something you already partly know. — [Golman & Loewenstein, "Information Gaps" (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Information%20Gaps--Golman%20and%20Loewenstein.pdf); [Golman & Loewenstein, curiosity paper (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science* 20(8):963-973), using 40 trivia questions, found an inverted-U between curiosity and confidence: little curiosity when people were very unsure or very sure of the answer, most in the middle. fMRI: curiosity tracked activity in caudate regions previously linked to anticipated reward; people would spend scarce tokens or waiting time to get answers they were curious about; higher curiosity predicted better recall of *surprising* answers 1-2 weeks later. Small sample (a secondary source says 19 students; unverified against primary). — [Kang et al. full text (CMU)](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19619181/)
- **[CONTESTED - largely debunked as a memory effect]** A 2025 meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects (Ghibellini & Meier, 59 publications) found **no overall memory advantage for unfinished tasks**; the Zeigarnik effect "lacks universal validity". The **Ovsiankina effect (tendency to resume an interrupted task) did hold as a general tendency**. A secondary summary gives the interrupted:completed recall ratio as 0.99 across 37 studies and a resumption rate of about 67% (both unverified against primary). — [RePEc record, Humanities & Social Sciences Communications 2025](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html); [Wikipedia: Zeigarnik effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect)
- **[MODERATE]** "The Teasing Effect" (Ruan, Hsee & Lu 2018, *Journal of Marketing Research* 55(4):556-570; seven studies): creating an uncertainty and then resolving it leaves people with a better experience than giving all the information at once, even though people say they would prefer to get everything up front. An ad built on a create-then-resolve structure improved attitude toward, and willingness to try, the product. — [University of Iowa record](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Teasing-Effect-An-Underappreciated-Benefit/9984380448802771); [Chicago Booth Review summary](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/how-hook-customers-first-tease-them)
- **[MODERATE]** "Pandora effect" (Hsee & Ruan 2016, *Psychological Science*): across four experiments, people exposed themselves to aversive stimuli (even electric shocks) just to resolve uncertainty, with no benefit. Curiosity drives the *click*, independent of whether the payoff is good. — [Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616631733)
- **[STRONG for headlines; transfer to video titles is inference]** How much to reveal: Aubin Le Quere & Matias (2025, *Scientific Reports*) built a continuous concreteness measure and analysed 27,616 real headline field experiments from a U.S. publisher (meta-analysis of 8,977 experiments). Result is **curvilinear**: when headlines were too vague, making them more concrete *raised* clicks; when already very concrete, more concreteness *lowered* clicks. "Just the right amount of information" wins at scale. The authors present this as the reconciliation of prior lab/field studies that conflicted. — [When curiosity gaps backfire (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11704130/)
- **[CONTESTED]** Scacco & Muddiman (2020, *New Media & Society*, "The curiosity effect") found summary headlines drew more engagement than some curiosity-style headlines in a lab experiment and a field test. — [record of the paper](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/1461444819863408)
- **[CONTESTED]** Banerjee & Urminsky's analysis of the Upworthy archive found textual cues from earlier research do affect headline performance, but prior research "does not provide useful guidance as to the direction of the effects" - i.e. headline formulas do not generalise reliably. — [Banerjee & Urminsky working paper](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf)
- **[STRONG, large-N field experiments, but 2013-2015 news headlines]** Upworthy Research Archive: 105,000+ headlines for 22,700+ stories, 5.7M clicks from 370M impressions; median headline CTR 1.07%. Negative words raised CTR (about +2.3% per Upworthy's summary), positive words lowered it (about -1.0%); in the *Nature Human Behaviour* analysis, sadness words helped more than anger or fear words. Effects are real but small. — [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/); [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4); [Cornell on the archive](https://news.cornell.edu/node/322110); [Upworthy's own response](https://www.upworthy.com/upworthy-negative-headlines-study/?nb=1)
- **[STRONG as neuroscience; LORE as usually applied]** Prediction error: dopamine neurons signal the difference between expected and received reward - a burst for an unexpected reward, almost nothing for a fully predicted one, a dip when an expected reward fails to arrive. But "dopamine hit" is a loose metaphor: dopamine is not "the pleasure chemical" (pleasure involves opioid and endocannabinoid systems), imaging studies measure brain activity not dopamine release, there is no evidence everyday scrolling causes a "dopamine overdose", and neither major clinical manual recognises social-media addiction as a diagnosis. — [PsyPost explainer](https://www.psypost.org/neuroscience-the-surprising-truth-about-dopamine/); [Harvard SITN (2018)](https://sitn.hms.harvard.edu/flash/2018/dopamine-smartphones-battle-time/); [Recovered.org](https://recovered.org/blog/dopamine-addiction-fact-or-clickbait); [The Spike: "The crimes against dopamine"](https://medium.com/the-spike/the-crimes-against-dopamine-b82b082d5f3d)

### Inferences
- **Practical: design the gap, don't just withhold.** The title/thumbnail should give enough concrete context that the viewer can name the specific thing they don't know ("which of these three failed and why"), not a vague tease ("you won't believe this"). Vague = bottom of the inverted-U. Fully answered in the title = other bottom.
- **Practical: a quick self-test for titles.** Ask "after reading this, can the viewer state the question the video answers?" If no, add concreteness. Ask "can they state the answer?" If yes, remove the answer but keep the stakes.
- **Practical: state the question in the first 5-15 seconds of the video, and close it.** The Teasing Effect supports create-then-resolve; nothing supports create-and-never-resolve. Open a main loop at the start, close it at the end, and open/close smaller loops along the way so resolution arrives regularly.
- **Practical: confirm the click immediately.** The Ovsiankina/resumption finding suggests the pull is to *continue something already begun*. Make the first shot visibly continue the thumbnail's promise so the viewer feels they have started the thing they clicked for.
- **Practical: surprise should come with the payoff.** Kang et al. suggests curiosity strengthens memory specifically for surprising answers, and prediction-error logic says a fully predictable payoff is flat. A reveal that is slightly different from what the viewer guessed is more memorable than one that confirms it.
- **Lore correction:** stop citing "the Zeigarnik effect" as scientific proof that open loops keep people watching. The honest version is: information-gap curiosity (moderate evidence) plus a tendency to resume unfinished things (supported) plus the teasing effect (moderate). Also drop "dopamine hit" language from any checklist; "reward prediction error" justifies *surprise and variability*, not claims about addiction.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment was found that manipulates the information gap in *YouTube titles/thumbnails specifically* and measures CTR plus retention. All the reveal-vs-withhold evidence is from news headlines and lab trivia.
- Loewenstein (1994) original paper (Psychological Bulletin 116(1):75-98) was not opened directly; description relies on later Golman & Loewenstein papers.
- Not sourced this session: Gruber et al. (2014) on curiosity states enhancing memory for incidental material; could support "curiosity makes surrounding content more memorable" if the writer wants it, but needs a citation check.

## 2. Emotion and sharing (Berger & Milkman, STEPPS, awe/anger/anxiety/humor; sharing vs watching vs commenting)

### Takeaway
The best-replicated finding is that **arousal, not just positivity, drives sharing**: high-arousal emotions (awe, amusement, excitement, anger, anxiety) increase sharing while low-arousal ones (sadness, contentment) do not, and emotional content beats informational content for sharing. But the emotional profile that earns a *click* (mild negativity/sadness in headlines), the one that *holds* attention (repeated pulses of joy, an early surprise), the one that drives *shares* (awe, inspiration, warmth, amusement), and the one that drives *comments/replies* (out-group anger, moral outrage) are not the same, and the last one costs trust.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Berger & Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research* 49:192-205): analysis of three months of New York Times articles plus controlled experiments. Positive content was shared more than negative, but the key driver was physiological arousal: content evoking high-arousal positive (awe) or negative (anger, anxiety) emotion was more viral; low-arousal emotion (sadness) was less viral. Experiments showed a causal effect driven by activation. Held after controlling for how surprising, interesting or practically useful the content was, and for placement. — [Berger & Milkman PDF (Wharton)](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2011/11/Virality.pdf); [Scientific American summary](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-secret-to-online-success-what-makes-content-go-viral/)
- **[STRONG, and specifically about YouTube video]** Tellis, MacInnis, Tirunillai & Zhang (2019, *Journal of Marketing* 83(4):1-20): two field studies of YouTube video ads (109 brands, 11 emotion measures, 60+ ad characteristics) plus a third out-of-sample validation. Positive emotions - inspiration, excitement, amusement, warmth - strongly increased sharing; drama/plot elements helped; **information-focused content had a significantly negative effect on sharing** except in risky (high-price / new product) contexts; prominent, early or long brand placement reduced sharing vs late placement; very short or very long ads were shared less. Advertisers nonetheless used information more than emotion and little surprise. — [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2019/06/07/the-hidden-secrets-of-creating-a-viral-youtube-ad/); [Phys.org](https://phys.org/news/2019-06-hidden-secrets-viral-youtube-ad.html); [AMA research insight](https://ama.org/research-insights/research-insight-what-drives-sharing-of-digital-content)
- **[MODERATE - small lab sample, n=58, 28 ads]** Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters (2012, *JMR* 49:144-159) tracked facial expressions, eye movements and "zapping" (skipping). **Surprise mainly concentrates attention; joy mainly keeps people from leaving.** For retention the *rate of change* of joy matters more than its level, and gains from rising joy exceed losses from falling joy; press coverage summarised the implication as an early surprise followed by several separate bursts of joy rather than one sustained stretch. — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/emotion-induced-engagement-in-internet-video-ads/); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/creating-online-ads-we-want-to-watch)
- **[STRONG, replicated with smaller effect]** Brady et al. (2017, *PNAS*; 563,312 tweets on polarising issues): each additional moral-emotional word increased retweets by about 20%. A 2025 pre-registered replication (N = 849,266) found about 12% per word - same direction, smaller. — [Brady et al. PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Jay-Van-Bavel/publication/317947723_Emotion_shapes_the_diffusion_of_moralized_content_in_social_networks/links/5f92152f458515b7cf96abb2/Emotion-shapes-the-diffusion-of-moralized-content-in-social-networks.pdf); [Kellogg: replication record](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2025/estimating-the-effect-size-of-moral-contagion-in-online/)
- **[STRONG for political content on Facebook/Twitter]** Rathje, Van Bavel & van der Linden (2021, *PNAS*; ~2.7M posts): each out-group term raised the odds of a share by about 67%; the out-group effect was about 4.8x the effect of negative-affect language and 6.7x that of moral-emotional language. Out-group posts mainly drew angry reactions. — [PNAS via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/8256037); [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/viralpolitics)
- **[STRONG for clicks on news]** Negativity raises click-through on headlines (Upworthy archive), with *sadness* words more effective than anger or fear - note this is the opposite of the sharing pattern, where sadness suppresses sharing. — [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4); [Nieman Lab](https://www.niemanlab.org/2023/03/negative-words-in-news-headlines-generate-more-clicks-but-sad-words-are-more-effective-than-angry-or-scary-ones/)
- **[MODERATE]** Outrage has a cost: Scacco & Muddiman's outrage-headline experiment found outrage headlines did not increase intended engagement and at worst backfired, prompting readers to suspect fake news (credibility 3.42 vs 3.49 out of 5 - a small gap). — [News Co/Lab summary](https://newscollab.org/2019/06/04/outrageous-clickbait-content-can-deter-long-term-engagement-study-finds/)

### Inferences
- **Emotional profile by goal (synthesis of the above; the mapping to watch/comment is inference, since most studies measure only sharing or only clicks):**
  - *Click:* a concrete curiosity gap, optionally with mild negative/loss framing. Small effect.
  - *Keep watching:* surprise early to lock attention; then repeated, separate peaks of positive emotion (humor, small wins, reveals). Emotional *change* holds people; a flat emotional line loses them even if it is pleasant.
  - *Share:* high-arousal positive - awe ("how is that possible"), inspiration, warmth, amusement - plus practical value in risky/expensive decisions. Pure information dumps are shared less.
  - *Comment:* anger, disagreement, out-group and moral framing reliably produce reactions and replies, but this is the profile most associated with distrust.
- **Practical:** in the script, mark each beat with its intended emotion and check that (a) arousal is high at the moments you want shared, (b) there is at least one awe/"wow" or genuinely funny moment that stands alone as a clip, (c) sponsor/brand mentions are not front-loaded - Tellis et al. found early and prolonged branding suppresses sharing (relevant for sponsor read placement, though a sponsor read inside a creator video was not what they tested).
- **Practical:** sad or calm content can still be watched and valued, but should not be expected to spread via shares; pair it with an activating ending (hope, resolve, awe) if sharing is a goal.

### Gaps
- STEPPS (Social currency, Triggers, Emotion, Public, Practical value, Stories) from Berger's book *Contagious* (2013) was not sourced this session. It is a practitioner framework that packages Berger's papers; treat it as a useful mnemonic, not as a tested model. No independent validation of STEPPS as a whole was found.
- Exact effect sizes from Berger & Milkman (commonly quoted as roughly +34% for a one-SD increase in anger and +30% for awe on the odds of making the most-emailed list) and the ~7,000-article sample size were not verified against the paper this session.
- No peer-reviewed study was found that compares the emotional drivers of *watching vs sharing vs commenting* on the same set of YouTube or TikTok videos. The comment/outrage evidence is from text posts on Twitter/Facebook, mostly political.
- Humor specifically: supported only indirectly (amusement in Tellis et al.; joy in Teixeira et al.). No dedicated source on humor and video sharing was retrieved.

## 3. Narrative (transportation, structure, stakes, "but/therefore", identification)

### Takeaway
Narrative transportation - being absorbed in a story - is a well-established, meta-analytically supported mechanism that changes attitudes and intentions, and complete dramatic structure correlates with more views and shares of online video. The "but/therefore" rule is craft advice, not research, but it is a compact way to enforce the causal coherence that the transportation literature says stories need.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** van Laer, de Ruyter, Visconti & Wetzels (2014, *Journal of Consumer Research* 40:797-817) meta-analysed 132 effect sizes from 76 articles on narrative transportation, building on Green & Brock's (2000) transportation scale ("Extended Transportation-Imagery Model": antecedents on the story side and the receiver side; consequences for affect, beliefs, attitudes, intentions). — [RePEc record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jconrs:doi:10.1086/673383); [City University open-access copy](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/6755/)
- **[STRONG]** A 2019 follow-up meta-analysis (van Laer, Feiereisen & Visconti, *Journal of Business Research*; 64 articles, 138 effect sizes) found the transportation effect is **stronger for user-generated content** and for commercial stories. — [record](https://katalog.lib.cas.cz/KNAV/EdsRecord/edsair,edsair.doi...........265472b3c4faaaa9698c0d6eb201b8a2)
- **[MODERATE - correlational content analysis, 155 viral ads]** Quesenberry & Coolsen (2019, *Journal of Interactive Marketing* 48:1-16, "Drama Goes Viral") coded viral video ads on Freytag's five-act structure (exposition, complication, climax, reversal, denouement). Four- and five-act videos averaged more than four times the shares of videos with zero to three acts; 66% of videos had three acts or fewer; only 25% were full five-act stories. Partial structures that had helped TV ad ratings did not help online. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joinma/v48y2019icp1-16.html); [Messiah University summary](https://www.messiah.edu/news/2019/11/for-brands-a-little-drama-is-a-good-thing)
- **[MODERATE]** Tellis et al. (2019) independently found that drama and plot elements were among the drivers of YouTube ad sharing. — [AMA summary](https://www.ama.org/2019/06/07/the-hidden-secrets-of-creating-a-viral-youtube-ad/)
- **[CRAFT ADVICE, not research]** Trey Parker and Matt Stone's rule, from a talk to an NYU writing class recorded for MTVU's "Stand In": if the words between your story beats are "and then", the story is weak; each beat should connect to the next with "therefore" or "but", so that causation and conflict carry the story. Sources disagree on the date of the talk (2011, 2014 and 2017 are all given). — [Cracked](https://cracked.com/article_43358_trey-parker-and-matt-stones-surprise-nyu-class-contains-some-great-writing-advice.html); [David Perell note](https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/); [CheatSheet](https://www.cheatsheet.com/entertainment/south-park-trey-parker-and-matt-stone-1-rule.html/)
- **[MODERATE]** Direct address and identification are separate levers: an experiment using *House of Cards* found that a character facing the camera and addressing viewers raised parasocial experience but did not change identification with the character. — [Penn State record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/the-differential-effects-of-direct-address-on-parasocial-experien/)

### Inferences
- **Practical:** even an informational video benefits from a story spine: a person who wants something (stake), an obstacle (but), a consequence (therefore), a turn, a resolution. Script test: read the beat list aloud; every transition that is "and then" is a likely retention dip - rewrite it as "but" (complication) or "therefore" (consequence).
- **Practical:** state the stake early and make it concrete (what is lost if this fails, by when). Stakes are what convert a curiosity gap into tension; tension-then-release is the narrative-scale version of the teasing effect in section 1.
- **Practical:** finish the story. The online-video evidence favours *complete* structures (climax plus resolution); videos that set up and stop are the low-share group in Quesenberry & Coolsen.
- **Practical:** creator-made, first-person stories are the format where transportation effects are strongest (UGC finding), which supports telling the story as your own experience rather than as detached narration.
- Looking into the camera builds the feeling of being spoken to; it does not by itself make viewers identify with you. Identification needs a goal and vulnerability the viewer can share.

### Gaps
- The pooled effect size (r) from van Laer et al. (2014) was not retrieved; cite as "meta-analytically supported" without a number unless checked.
- Green & Brock (2000, *JPSP* 79(5):701-721) and Cohen (2001) on identification were not opened this session; they are described only through later work.
- No controlled study was found testing "but/therefore" causal structure against "and then" structure on video retention. It is plausible and consistent with theory, but unproven.
- Quesenberry & Coolsen sampled already-viral ads, so the result cannot say that adding acts *causes* virality.

## 4. Attention and cognitive load (Mayer, pacing, cuts, orienting response, captions, drop-off curves, attention-span myths)

### Takeaway
There is no fixed human "attention span" and the 8-second goldfish number is fabricated; attention depends on the task and on whether the content keeps earning it. What research does support: cut extraneous material, signal what matters, and segment (Mayer); within-scene edits raise arousal and memory up to a point (Lang); a large share of mobile viewing is sound-off or captioned; and the biggest audience loss happens in the opening seconds, which is why YouTube reports a dedicated 30-second "intro" retention figure. There is no research-backed optimal cut frequency.

### Cited Findings
- **[LORE - debunked]** The "8-second attention span, shorter than a goldfish" claim comes from a graphic on page six of a 2015 Microsoft Canada report for marketers (12 seconds in 2000, 8 in 2013, goldfish 9). The figures were not Microsoft's research; they were sourced to a commercial site called Statistic Brain, and goldfish were in no sample. The BBC's 2017 investigation concluded there is no evidence human attention spans are shrinking and none that goldfish have short ones; researchers note attention depends on the task. A 2022 King's College London survey found half of UK adults still believed it. — [BBC article (copy hosted by UW-Madison)](https://internet.psych.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/532-Master/532-UnitPages/Unit-09/Goldfish_MythBusting.pdf); [Temple Law](https://law.temple.edu/aer/?p=3749); [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/t-humans-eight-second-attention-span-goldfish-myth/); [Medium: persistence and KCL survey](https://medium.com/write-rise/the-attention-span-stat-everyone-keeps-getting-wrong-91c418793ccc)
- **[MODERATE - real research, often misquoted]** Gloria Mark (UC Irvine) reports that average time on a single screen before switching fell from about 2.5 minutes (2003-04 study) to about 47 seconds (studies 2016-2020), and that people interrupt themselves about half the time. This measures task-switching at a computer, not a capacity limit on attention to a video. — [UC Newsroom interview](https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/how-sharpen-your-attention-and-meet-your-goals-2024)
- **[CONTESTED / correlational]** A 2025 *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis (Nguyen et al., 71 studies, 98,299 participants) linked heavier short-form video use to poorer cognition (r = -.34 overall; attention r = -.38; inhibitory control r = -.41) and weaker links to mental health (about r = -.21). Most studies were cross-sectional, and the strong associations were with addiction-like use rather than raw viewing time; some coverage called it "brain rot", others argued the effects are modest. It does not show that short video shortens attention. — [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/large-meta-analysis-links-tiktok-and-instagram-reels-to-poorer-cognitive-and-mental-health/); [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2025/journal-article-abstracts/bul0000498/); [Reason critique](https://reason.com/2025/12/31/study-short-form-video-isnt-rotting-your-brain)
- **[STRONG for learning outcomes]** Mayer's multimedia principles, median effect sizes from his own research corpus: **coherence** (delete extraneous words, pictures, sounds) d about 0.86 (18 of 19 tests positive); **signaling** (highlight the essential) d about 0.70 in one Mayer table; **segmenting** (learner-paced chunks) d about 0.77-0.79 (10 of 10). Independent meta-analyses find smaller but real effects: signaling d = 0.38 overall; segmenting small-to-medium for retention and transfer. Figures vary by edition of Mayer's reviews. — [Mayer, research-based principles (UNH copy)](https://www.unh.edu/teaching-learning-resource-hub/sites/default/files/media/2023-06/itow-research-based-principles-for-designing-multimedia-instruction-mayer.pdf); [signaling meta-analysis](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-meta-analysis-of-signaling-principle-in/99900601052901842); [segmenting meta-analysis](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4)
- **[STRONG for educational video; large N]** Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014, ACM Learning@Scale): 6.9 million video-watching sessions on edX. Shorter videos were far more engaging (recommendation: segments under 6 minutes); videos mixing a talking head with slides beat slides alone; informal, hand-drawn tablet tutorials beat slides and screencasts; chopped-up classroom lectures did poorly even when high quality; instructors who spoke fairly fast with high enthusiasm were more engaging. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin PDF (MIT)](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- **[MODERATE - TV, lab, 2000]** Lang et al. (2000, *Journal of Broadcasting & Electronic Media* 44(1):94-109) varied the rate of edits (camera changes within the same scene) from slow to very fast: as edit rate rose, physiological arousal, self-reported arousal and memory all rose, and the authors concluded edits can raise attention and encoding without significantly raising cognitive load. Underlying mechanism: novel stimuli that do not match the current mental model elicit an orienting response (Sokolov). — [CityU record with abstract](https://scholars.cityu.edu.hk/en/publications/the-effects-of-edits-on-arousal-attention-and-memory-for-televisi/)
- **[STRONG as a descriptive trend]** Average shot length in English-language films fell from about 12 seconds in 1930 to about 2.5 seconds in recent films (Cutting and colleagues; 150-160 films, 1935-2010); the authors read this as film-makers taking increasing control of viewers' attention. Descriptive only - it does not show shorter shots are better. — [Cutting et al., i-Perception](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1068/i0441aap); [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3485803)
- **[CONTESTED]** Claims that fast pacing damages attention rest on children's TV studies that conflict: a 2011 study found worse executive function after a fast-paced cartoon; a 2024 systematic review calls the findings conflicting; 2025 work reported that fantastical content, not editing speed, was the stronger factor. — [BMC Psychology systematic review](https://bmcpsychology.biomedcentral.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s40359-024-01738-1.pdf); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/fantastical-content-not-editing-speed-depletes-childrens-cognitive-resources/)
- **[INDUSTRY / survey - weak but directionally consistent]** Sound-off viewing: "85% of Facebook video is watched without sound" is 2016 publisher-reported data (e.g. LittleThings), not a study. A Verizon Media / Publicis Media survey of U.S. consumers reported 92% view video with sound off on mobile and 80% are more likely to watch a whole video when captions are available. Both are old, Facebook-feed-era figures; TikTok/Reels/Shorts default to sound-on. — [Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/); [NextTV](https://nexttv.com/news/mobile-videos-often-watched-without-audio-study-finds)
- **[INDUSTRY survey]** Preply (2022, about 1,260 Americans): half watch content with subtitles most of the time; 70% of Gen Z; top reason (72%) is that subtitles make dialogue easier to understand. Self-reported, by a language-learning company. — [Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/americas-subtitles-use)
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube Studio's "key moments for audience retention" report includes an **Intro** metric: the percentage of the audience still watching after the first 30 seconds. It also flags spikes (rewatched/shared moments), dips (skipped/abandoned moments) and compares to typical retention for videos of similar length. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9314415?hl=en)
- **[INDUSTRY - mutually inconsistent]** Long-form benchmarks: vidIQ calls about 60% still watching at 30 seconds good and about 30% average-percentage-viewed typical for long-form (its own channel: 30.3% over 16.5M long-form views; 73.6% average percentage viewed on its Shorts); another tool blog gives 50-70% at 30 seconds; another says an intro "needs work" if more than 40% leave. Wistia (business-hosted video) reports an early drop of 4.9% in the first 2% of 1-2 minute videos vs 17.3% for 5-10 minute videos. A "2025 Retention Rabbit Benchmark Report" (10,000+ videos) is quoted second-hand as 23.7% average retention, about 31.5% for 5-10 minute videos, and 55% of viewers gone within 60 seconds - not found at source and internally inconsistent in the secondary page. — [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/); [Async](https://async.com/blog/audience-retention-challenges/); [Async citing Wistia](https://async.com/blog/why-videos-lose-engagement/); [Opus](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-retention-graphs-explained); [Stripo citing Retention Rabbit](https://research.stripo.email/youtube-benchmarks)

### Inferences
- **Practical (coherence):** in the edit, remove anything that does not serve the promise in the title - decorative b-roll that fights the narration, tangents, long logo intros, music with lyrics under speech. This is the best-supported edit rule in the list.
- **Practical (signaling):** put on screen only the 2-5 key words of a point, highlight/zoom the part of the frame that matters, and use spoken signposts ("three things; first..."). Avoid duplicating full sentences as on-screen text while the same words are spoken.
- **Practical (segmenting):** build the video in clearly marked chunks with a mini-payoff at the end of each (and chapters). For teaching content, think in units of a few minutes.
- **Practical (pacing):** visual change (cut, angle change, zoom, graphic, location) renews attention through the orienting response, and Lang's data suggest more within-scene edits help rather than overload - when the cuts are *related* to the content. But there is **no evidence for a specific magic interval** ("cut every 3 seconds"); that is lore. Use change to mark a new idea, and let the pace vary - habituation applies to constant fast cutting as much as to a static shot.
- **Practical (captions):** assume a meaningful share of viewers start muted or read along. Burn in captions for short-form; make the first seconds understandable from picture plus text alone; keep caption chunks short and synced.
- **Practical (opening):** the first 30 seconds is the steepest part of every retention curve and the only part YouTube reports as a headline number. No channel idents, no "hey guys welcome back", no recap of what the title already said: confirm the promise, show the stake, start.
- Benchmarks: compare against your own videos of similar length (YouTube's own "typical" comparison does this). Do not adopt any third-party percentage as a target.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests cut frequency or "pattern interrupts" on retention of YouTube or TikTok videos. Lang's work is 1990s-2000 television with within-scene edits; her 1993 paper on related vs unrelated cuts was identified but its findings were not retrieved.
- No current (2024-2026), methodologically transparent measurement of sound-off viewing share on TikTok / Reels / Shorts was found. The widely quoted 85% and 92% figures are 2016-2019 and Facebook/mobile-web era.
- Gernsbacher's (2015) review of captions and comprehension was not retrieved; the claim that captions improve comprehension and memory for hearing viewers is plausible but unsourced here.
- The Retention Rabbit report itself was not located; treat every number attributed to it as unverified.
- A pooled meta-analysis of Mayer's own corpus (reported in search extracts as overall g about 0.37 with a slight decline over time) could not be pinned to a specific source; candidate records are [NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10637927) and [PMC7940870](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7940870). Mayer's medians come largely from his own lab's short lab lessons and likely overstate real-world effects.

## 5. Parasocial relationships, authenticity, direct address, and why viewers return

### Takeaway
Viewers return to a *person* more than to a topic: parasocial relationships are a robust, meta-analytically supported phenomenon, and direct address (looking into the lens, speaking to "you") experimentally increases the feeling of interaction. Self-disclosure and perceived similarity are associated with stronger parasocial bonds in YouTuber studies, but that evidence is mostly cross-sectional surveys, and "authenticity drives loyalty" is plausible rather than proven. Habit and "variable reward" explanations for returning are theory-driven; direct evidence for creator channels is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG]** Tukachinsky, Walter & Saucier (2020, *Journal of Communication*): meta-analysis of four decades of research (k = 120) on antecedents and effects of parasocial relationships (PSRs). PSRs and in-the-moment parasocial *interaction* are only moderately correlated (distinct constructs), and **no significant relationship was found between social deficiencies (e.g. loneliness) and PSRs** - the "parasocial bonds are for lonely people" idea is not supported. — [record of the meta-analysis](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/40aDeVml)
- **[MODERATE - experimental]** Hartmann & Goldhoorn (2011, *Journal of Communication* 61(6):1104-1121, "Horton and Wohl revisited") introduced the Experience of Parasocial Interaction scale and found viewers reported a more intense parasocial experience when the performer addressed them directly (bodily and verbal address). — [paper record](https://typeset.io/papers/horton-and-wohl-revisited-exploring-viewers-experience-of-2x3qk052xt)
- **[MODERATE]** Atad & Cohen tested whether a presenter's direct address affects viewers' parasocial interaction and perceived credibility ("Look me in the eyes"). — [University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/look-me-in-the-eyes-how-direct-address-affects-viewers-experience/)
- **[WEAK-MODERATE - survey]** A 2022 study in *Sustainability* found a YouTuber's degree of self-disclosure, perceived similarity to the viewer, and attractiveness each had a significant positive association with viewers' parasocial interaction, which in turn related to purchase intention. — [Sustainability 15(1):550](https://www.doi.org/10.3390/su15010550)
- **[WEAK - qualitative]** A 2023 focus-group study in *Current Psychology* reported that a YouTuber's disclosure of personal information evoked a sense of connection and "invited a friendly relationship". — [Current Psychology](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12144-023-04784-x.pdf)
- **[MODERATE]** Guo, Kim & Rubin's edX data point the same way for instructional video: informal, personal, enthusiastic delivery out-performed polished studio/lecture footage. — [Guo, Kim & Rubin PDF](https://up.csail.mit.edu/other-pubs/las2014-pguo-engagement.pdf)
- **[STRONG as neuroscience, LORE as applied]** Variable reward: reward-prediction-error research supports that unexpected rewards produce a stronger signal than predicted ones, and social media's unpredictable rewards are discussed as a plausible engagement mechanism - but this is an argument by analogy, not a measured effect of creator behaviour. — [Harvard SITN](https://sitn.hms.harvard.edu/flash/2018/dopamine-smartphones-battle-time/); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/neuroscience-the-surprising-truth-about-dopamine/)
- **[STRONG]** Mere exposure: Montoya et al. (2017, *Psychological Bulletin*; 268 curve estimates from 81 articles) found liking rises with repeated exposure but with a negative quadratic term - an inverted-U, i.e. familiarity builds liking until satiation/boredom sets in. — [Murdoch University record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-re-examination-of-the-mere-exposure/991005635870307891)

### Inferences
- **Practical:** look into the lens and address a single viewer ("you"), especially in the first seconds and at emotional beats. This is the one parasocial technique with experimental support.
- **Practical:** include small, real self-disclosures (what you got wrong, what you were worried about, why you care). Keep them relevant to the video; disclosure is associated with closeness, but the evidence does not support oversharing as a tactic.
- **Practical:** consistency builds familiarity (mere exposure) - recurring format, voice, visual identity, running jokes, a predictable schedule - but vary the content inside the format, because the exposure curve turns down with repetition. "Same show, new episode."
- **Practical:** to make viewing a habit, give the viewer a stable cue and a reliable reward (same day/time, recognisable packaging, consistent delivery on the promise), with the surprise coming from *what happens*, not from whether the video delivers.
- "Authenticity" is best operationalised as: unscripted-sounding delivery, visible imperfection, and a match between what the title promises and what you actually do. Polished production is not what the evidence rewards.

### Gaps
- Horton & Wohl's original 1956 paper was not retrieved; it is referenced only through later work.
- No study was found that measures *subscriber retention or return-viewing* as an outcome of authenticity or self-disclosure. A 2017 conference paper on loyalty toward beauty YouTubers from a parasocial perspective was identified in a reference list but not retrieved.
- Habit formation and variable-ratio reinforcement as reasons viewers return to a specific creator: no direct empirical source found. Likewise identity/community ("being a fan of X as part of who I am") - theoretically grounded in social identity theory but not sourced in this session.
- No experiment specific to YouTubers on eye contact / direct address was found; the direct-address evidence is from TV presenters and drama.

## 6. Social proof, mere exposure, FOMO, loss aversion, and the peak-end rule (how to end a video)

### Takeaway
Social proof and the peak-end rule are strongly supported; mere exposure is supported with a satiation ceiling; FOMO is a real, measurable individual difference; loss aversion is real in many settings but its universality is actively disputed, so "people hate losing twice as much as they like winning" should not be stated as a law. For endings, the meta-analytic evidence says both the most intense moment and the final moment heavily shape how the whole video is remembered - but the average quality matters about as much, so a strong ending cannot rescue a weak middle.

### Cited Findings
- **[STRONG - experimental, replicated]** Salganik, Dodds & Watts (2006, *Science*): in the "Music Lab" multiple-worlds experiment, participants who could see others' download counts produced markets with more inequality and more unpredictability of success than participants choosing independently; a 2009 follow-up found similar results with a different pool. Visible popularity changes what people choose, and success becomes partly luck-driven. — [Princeton Music Lab page](https://www.princeton.edu/~mjs3/musiclab.shtml); [Science paper (copy)](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~bor/303s19/docs/salganik_dodds_watts.pdf)
- **[STRONG]** Mere exposure follows an inverted-U (Montoya et al. 2017, 81 articles). — [Murdoch University record](https://researchportal.murdoch.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/A-re-examination-of-the-mere-exposure/991005635870307891)
- **[MODERATE]** FOMO was first conceptualised and measured by Przybylski et al. (2013, *Computers in Human Behavior* 29:1841-1848): apprehension that others are having rewarding experiences without you, plus a desire to stay continually connected; associated with social-media engagement. — [Przybylski et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2013.02.014)
- **[CONTESTED]** Loss aversion: Gal & Rucker (2018, *Journal of Consumer Psychology*) argued the evidence does not support loss aversion as a general psychological principle, and that classic demonstrations may reflect inertia. A Stanford commentary agreed it is "less robust and universal than has been assumed" while rejecting the strongest version of the critique. Debate continues. — [Undark](https://undark.org/2021/11/15/among-social-scientists-a-vigorous-debate-over-loss-aversion/); [Stanford GSB commentary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/bringing-contingent-loss-aversion-down-earth-comment-gal-ruckers)
- **[STRONG - for clicks, small effect]** Negative wording raised headline CTR in 105,000+ Upworthy headline variations - consistent with negativity/loss framing drawing attention, though the effect is a couple of percent. — [Nature Human Behaviour](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-023-01538-4)
- **[STRONG]** Peak-end rule: Alaybek et al. (2022, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*) meta-analysed 174 effect sizes and found a large peak-end effect on retrospective evaluations (r = 0.581, 95% CI 0.487-0.661), stronger than trend or variability effects. However, the **overall average of the experience predicted evaluations about as well**, which undercuts the strong "duration neglect" reading. — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html); [Wikipedia: peak-end rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak%E2%80%93end_rule)
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube measures post-viewing satisfaction via surveys (1-5 stars; only 4-5 star responses count as "valued watch time") and trains a model to predict it, because raw watch time does not guarantee viewers feel good afterwards. How a video is remembered therefore feeds recommendations. — [YouTube Official Blog (Goodrow)](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-provides-new-overview-of-how-its-video-recommendation-systems-work/598586/)

### Inferences
- **Practical (ending):** end at, or immediately after, the strongest payoff. Do not trail off into a long outro, a recap of what was just shown, or a block of housekeeping - the last thing felt is weighted heavily in memory and in satisfaction ratings, and a slow fade-out is also where the retention curve collapses. Make any call to action brief and place it either just before the final payoff or folded into it.
- **Practical (peak):** deliberately build one clear high point (the reveal, the biggest laugh, the best shot) and protect it - give it space, do not bury it under a sponsor read.
- **Practical (social proof):** real numbers and real reactions (how many people tried this, a clip of someone's reaction, a pinned comment) help; early engagement compounds. The Music Lab result also means outcomes are partly luck - judge a format over many videos, not one.
- **Practical (loss framing / FOMO):** framing the stake as something the viewer could lose or miss ("the mistake that costs you X") can lift clicks slightly. Use it only when the loss is real and the video addresses it; treat the "2x" loss-aversion multiplier as unreliable.
- Invented urgency and scarcity are the point where this section crosses the ethical line in section 9.

### Gaps
- No study was found applying the peak-end rule to online video endings or to YouTube satisfaction specifically; the application is by analogy (the original work is on pain, sounds, and service experiences).
- Kahneman's original peak-end papers and the Brown et al. meta-analysis of the loss-aversion coefficient were not retrieved.
- No evidence was found on whether end-screen placement or outro length causally changes satisfaction or return viewing.

## 7. Thumbnails and titles (faces, emotion, contrast, simplicity, word count; A/B data)

### Takeaway
There is no peer-reviewed causal evidence on thumbnail features and CTR; everything available is industry description of already-successful videos, and the data sets disagree. The most defensible reading is that faces, exaggerated expressions and bright contrast are *common*, not *causal* - one analysis found breakout and underperforming thumbnails look almost identical - while short titles and little or no thumbnail text are the most consistent patterns. YouTube's own A/B tool deliberately picks winners by watch time, not CTR, which is the single most important design fact here.

### Cited Findings
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube's "Test & Compare" (A/B testing in Studio) lets a creator test up to three thumbnails, titles, or title+thumbnail combinations on one video; variants are shown randomly to viewers; the winner is chosen by **watch time share**, not click-through rate, because YouTube says this "will best support creators' growth". Results are labelled Winner (statistically significant) or Preferred (likely better, not conclusive). Title testing was added in 2025; tests run up to about two weeks. — [YouTube Help: test & compare thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714); [YouTube Help: A/B test titles & thumbnails](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16391400?hl=en); [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-title-a-b-testing-rolls-out-globally-to-creators)
- **[PLATFORM-adjacent]** A YouTube creator-liaison explanation quoted in coverage: if you over-index on CTR it can become clickbait, which can tank retention and hurt performance. — [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/do-faces-help-youtube-thumbnails-heres-what-the-data-says/563944/)
- **[INDUSTRY]** vidIQ thumbnail study (published mid-2026; 500 "breakout" videos - far more views than the channel's own average - across 30 niches): a human face appeared in 69% of all 500, 75% of the top 100 and 80% of the top 50. High contrast appeared in 56% and did not rise with performance (treated as a baseline, not a differentiator). **Exaggerated expressions were rare: about 5% overall, 6% of the top 50.** Text: 140 had no text, 120 had 1-3 words, 84 had 4-5, 63 had 6-7, 34 had 8-9, 59 had 10+ (so 52% had three words or fewer). vidIQ states the study shows what breakout thumbnails used, not what caused the performance. — [vidIQ Research](https://vidiq.com/research/youtube-thumbnail-study/)
- **[INDUSTRY - conflicting]** Eden analysed 507,478 videos and 2,960 thumbnails and found breakout thumbnails "look almost identical to underperformers": 84.2% of breakout thumbnails had a face, but so did 82.2% of underperformers. — [Eden](https://eden.so/blog/what-makes-a-good-youtube-thumbnail/)
- **[INDUSTRY - conflicting]** 1of10's 2025 data set (reported as about 300,000-323,000 high-performing videos, 50,000+ channels, 62.6B views; "outlier" = video views divided by channel median views): thumbnails with and without faces performed similarly overall; multiple faces did better than a single face; faces helped in some niches (e.g. finance) and hurt in others (e.g. business). Secondary write-ups report titles of about 5-6 words or fewer performing best, with performance falling beyond 6 words, and titles containing numbers getting about 11% fewer views (unverified against primary). — [Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/do-faces-help-youtube-thumbnails-heres-what-the-data-says/563944/); [1of10 blog](https://1of10.com/blog/what-actually-makes-a-youtube-video-go-viral-in-2025); [secondary write-up](https://quasa.io/media/youtube-s-biggest-thumbnail-title-study-ever-323-000-videos-62-6-billion-views-and-the-brutal-truth-about-what-actually-gets-clicks)
- **[INDUSTRY - descriptive]** Kapwing (2024) used Amazon Rekognition to classify the dominant face in top YouTubers' thumbnails: surprise and happiness each appeared in nearly 27%; positive emotions were more common than negative. It measured what big creators use, not what earns clicks; small sample, automated classifier. — [Kapwing](https://kapwing.com/resources/the-youtube-thumbnail-faces-that-attract-the-most-views); [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2024/10/24/emotion-in-thumbnails-ai-study-kapwing/)
- **[INDUSTRY]** A study of 100k channels was headlined as finding shocked faces on just 1 in 10 thumbnails. — [Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/youtube-thumbnail-study-of-100k-channels-finds-shocked-faces-on-just-1-in-10-covers/)
- **[STRONG - from headlines, by analogy]** For titles: the right amount of concreteness beats both vague and fully-revealing wording (27,616 headline experiments), and headline "formulas" do not transfer reliably across contexts. — [When curiosity gaps backfire](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11704130/); [Banerjee & Urminsky](https://home.uchicago.edu/ourminsky/Banerjee_Urminsky_Headlines.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Lore correction:** "always put a shocked face on the thumbnail" is not supported. Faces are common on winners *and* losers; exaggerated expressions are rare among breakouts in the newest data. A face is a reasonable default for personality-led channels (it supports the parasocial mechanism in section 5), not a CTR law.
- **Practical:** one idea per thumbnail, readable at phone size in under a second; zero to three words of text that *add to* the title rather than repeat it; high contrast as a baseline; title around six words or fewer with the concrete subject up front (these are the most consistent industry patterns, all correlational).
- **Practical:** title and thumbnail should together open exactly one gap (section 1) and the thumbnail image should be something the viewer will actually see in the video (section 9).
- **Practical:** test rather than follow rules. Use Test & Compare with 2-3 genuinely different concepts (not colour tweaks), accept that the winner is chosen on watch time share, and log results per channel - niche effects dominate the cross-channel averages.
- Because the platform optimises thumbnails on watch time, a thumbnail that over-promises will lose the test even if it wins clicks.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study relating thumbnail features (faces, emotion, colour, text) to CTR was found in this session; a direct statement to that effect also came back from the search. All numbers above are vendor analyses with survivorship bias (they mostly sample winners) and no control for topic or channel.
- YouTube has not published aggregate Test & Compare findings (which kinds of thumbnails tend to win). None found.
- The primary 1of10 report could not be opened; word-count and numbers-in-titles figures are second-hand.
- No reliable data on colour choice, text size or composition effects beyond "contrast is common".

## 8. Short-form specifics (first 1-3 seconds, loops/rewatches, swipe behaviour)

### Takeaway
In a swipe feed there is no thumbnail or title click: the opening frames *are* the packaging, and the viewer's decision to stay or swipe is the main signal the platform gets, which is why the hook carries disproportionate weight. Platforms confirm they measure stay-vs-swipe and completion; they do not publish thresholds, and every specific benchmark ("70% viewed", "3-second rule") is creator- or vendor-sourced.

### Cited Findings
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube Studio reports "Viewed (vs. swiped away)" for Shorts: the percentage of times viewers watched a Short versus swiped away. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12220281)
- **[INDUSTRY - no official benchmark]** YouTube publishes no benchmark for viewed-vs-swiped or any other Shorts metric. Third-party targets conflict (70-79% "decent", 80%+ "strong"; others say 75-80%; unsourced claims of 65% or 85%). Guides warn against believing a fixed percentage triggers a distribution cutoff and recommend comparing against your own Shorts of similar length/topic. — [Creator Essentials glossary](https://www.creatoressentials.com/glossary/viewed-vs-swiped-away/); [Bytecap](https://www.bytecap.io/research/youtube-shorts-retention-benchmarks); [1of10](https://1of10.com/blog/youtube-shorts-analytics/)
- **[PLATFORM, reported by press]** Since 31 March 2025 a Shorts "view" counts every time a Short starts playing or replays, with no minimum watch time (aligning with TikTok and Reels). The old, stricter measure survives as "engaged views" in Analytics and is still what monetisation and Partner Program eligibility use. So loops inflate public view counts but not the metric that pays. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted); [Digital Music News](https://www.digitalmusicnews.com/2025/03/27/how-does-youtube-shorts-count-views-2025/)
- **[PLATFORM]** TikTok's ad guidance: introduce your proposition in the first 3 seconds for better recall and awareness; prioritise the hook in the first 6 seconds to boost engagement and watch time; playbooks call the first 3-6 seconds critical. Ads can run up to 60 seconds; TikTok says 21-34 seconds performs best. — [TikTok Ads Help: creative best practices](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices); [TikTok video advertising guide](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en/guides/video-advertising-guide)
- **[INDUSTRY / unverified]** Widely repeated: "71% of TikTok users decide within the first three seconds" and "over 63% of the highest-CTR videos highlight the key message in the first three seconds" (the latter attributed to TikTok's auction ads guide). Neither could be verified against a TikTok document. — [Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/viral-tiktok-hooks-examples/)
- **[PLATFORM]** TikTok says its For You ranking predicts how likely you are to enjoy a video, and that user interactions - which may include time spent watching - are weighted more heavily than other factors. Coverage of TikTok's 2020 explainer lists watching a video from beginning to end, sharing, and following the creator as strong signals. — [TikTok transparency: recommendation system](https://www.tiktok.com/transparency/recommendation-system/); [TikTok Newsroom (2020)](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/how-tiktok-recommends-videos-for-you); [Tech Brew](https://www.techbrew.com/stories/2020/06/22/tiktok-provides-details-recommendation-system)
- **[INDUSTRY]** Shorts retention runs much higher than long-form (vidIQ: 73.6% average percentage viewed on its own Shorts) and the two should not be compared. — [vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/increase-audience-retention-youtube/)
- **[MODERATE - lab, ads]** The closest academic evidence for an early hook: surprise concentrates attention, and rising joy reduces skipping (Teixeira, Wedel & Pieters 2012). — [Tilburg University record](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/emotion-induced-engagement-in-internet-video-ads/)

### Inferences
- **Why the hook matters more in short-form (inference from the mechanics above):** in long-form the viewer has already chosen the video via title and thumbnail, so the opening only has to *confirm* the choice. In a swipe feed nothing was chosen; the first second must do the thumbnail's job (stop the thumb), the title's job (state the gap) and the intro's job (confirm value), and swiping costs zero effort. Stay-vs-swipe is also the first thing the system learns about the video.
- **Practical:** frame one is the thumbnail. Start mid-action or on the most striking visual; put the hook as on-screen text in the first frame (works muted); say the gap or the result in the first sentence. No greeting, no logo, no "so today".
- **Practical:** one idea per Short; cut every beat that does not move toward the payoff; deliver the payoff as late as possible but deliver it.
- **Practical (loops):** ending on the payoff with the last line flowing back into the first, and with no outro, encourages rewatching and removes the "it's over" cue that prompts a swipe. Useful, but note the YouTube public view count now counts replays regardless, so judge by engaged views and average percentage viewed, not raw views.
- **Practical:** length should follow the idea. Completion is easier when shorter, but platforms describe time-watched and completion as signals, not a specific duration.
- Rewatching as a ranking signal is plausible but appears only in third-party guides, not in the platform statements found here.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of swipe-feed drop-off timing (how fast viewers decide) was found. The "1-3 seconds" window is platform advice plus creator consensus, not a measured psychological constant.
- No official statement was found from TikTok, Instagram or YouTube that rewatches/loops are weighted in ranking, nor any official numeric threshold for any short-form metric.
- Instagram Reels-specific guidance was not researched in this session.
- TikTok's newsroom explainer wording was taken from secondary coverage; the exact current signal list was not confirmed.

## 9. The ethical line: engagement technique vs manipulative clickbait

### Takeaway
The line is whether the video delivers what the title and thumbnail promised. Curiosity gaps, stakes and emotional framing are legitimate when they point at something the video really contains; they become manipulative when the promise is false, exaggerated, or withheld. This is not only an ethical point: platforms explicitly optimise for post-view satisfaction rather than clicks, YouTube has begun removing egregious clickbait, and experiments suggest outrage/clickbait framing lowers credibility and can depress engagement.

### Cited Findings
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube moved from views to watch time in 2012 after feeds filled with sensational, off-putting videos, then added satisfaction: "valued watch time" is measured with viewer surveys (1-5 stars; only 4-5 count) and a model trained to predict the answers, because the company did not want viewers regretting time spent. A YouTube product manager has said watch time alone is imperfect since time spent does not guarantee viewers feel good afterwards. — [YouTube Official Blog](https://blog.youtube/inside-youtube/on-youtubes-recommendation-system/); [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/youtube-provides-new-overview-of-how-its-video-recommendation-systems-work/598586/); [Arab News / Bloomberg](https://www.arabnews.com/node/1200956)
- **[PLATFORM]** YouTube's A/B tool chooses thumbnail and title winners on watch time share rather than CTR. — [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13861714)
- **[PLATFORM]** December 2024: YouTube announced stronger enforcement against "egregious clickbait", defined as a title or thumbnail that "includes promises or claims that aren't delivered within the video itself", especially for breaking news and current events. Rollout began in India, initially removing violating videos without a strike and prioritising new uploads, with expansion to more countries stated as planned. — [Google India Blog](https://blog.google/intl/en-in/products/platforms/strengthening-enforcement-against-egregious-clickbait-on-youtube/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/12/18/youtube-says-it-will-start-cracking-down-on-videos-with-clickbait-titles-in-india)
- **[MODERATE]** Outrage headlines in Scacco & Muddiman's experiment did not raise intended engagement and at worst backfired, leading readers to suspect fake news; question-style headlines also lowered expected engagement relative to traditional ones. — [News Co/Lab](https://newscollab.org/2019/06/04/outrageous-clickbait-content-can-deter-long-term-engagement-study-finds/); [Journalism.co.uk](https://journalism.co.uk/ampnews/readers-perceive-question-based-headlines-more-negatively-study-shows/s435/a663096)
- **[WEAK - thesis]** A master's thesis found clickbait headlines reduce perceived source credibility, with already low-credibility sources hurt the most. — [thesis record](https://core.ac.uk/download/75905969.pdf)
- **[CONTESTED]** The evidence is not one-sided: at least one computational study reported clickbait receiving *more* engagement, and a 2023 study found exposure to clickbait headlines can still raise belief in their content. Clickbait can work in the short run. — [Penn State PIKE lab (CHI 2021)](https://pike.psu.edu/publications/chi21.pdf); [Exaggerated and questioning clickbait headlines (Hogrefe)](https://econtent.hogrefe.com/doi/10.1027/1864-1105/a000298)
- **[MODERATE]** Curiosity can pull people toward content regardless of payoff quality (people chose electric shocks to resolve uncertainty) - the mechanism that makes empty clickbait effective at getting the click is real. — [Hsee & Ruan 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797616631733)
- **[STRONG]** Outrage and out-group hostility do drive shares and reactions (out-group language +67% odds of a share per term), which the authors describe as a perverse incentive. — [University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/viralpolitics)

### Inferences
- **A workable test for the checklist ("promise-payoff audit"):**
  1. Write down the literal promise made by the title + thumbnail.
  2. Find the timestamp where the video delivers it. If there is none, change the packaging or the video.
  3. Is the payoff at least as good as a reasonable viewer would expect from the packaging? If the viewer's likely reaction at the end is "that's it?", it is clickbait even if technically true.
  4. Is the thumbnail image something that actually appears or happens in the video?
  5. Is any urgency, scarcity, danger or conflict in the framing real?
  6. Is the answer withheld for longer than the story needs? Delay that serves tension is craft; padding to hit a length is manipulation.
- **Legitimate vs manipulative, in one line each:** *legitimate* = selecting and framing the most interesting true thing about the video; *manipulative* = implying something the video does not contain, manufacturing outrage or out-group hostility for reactions, fake urgency, or teasing a payoff that never arrives.
- Short-term vs long-term: clickbait can raise clicks (curiosity operates independently of payoff), but the platform's objective is satisfaction-weighted watch time and the audience's response is lower trust - so the over-promise is penalised twice: once by the viewer who leaves early and rates the video poorly, and again in whether they click the same creator next time.
- Outrage and out-group framing sit in the same category: they reliably produce shares and comments, and they are the engagement lever most clearly associated with distrust.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study was found that measures the effect of clickbait on *return viewing* or subscriber churn for video creators; the "hurts long-term trust" claim rests on credibility experiments with news headlines plus platform design statements.
- Current status (late 2026) of YouTube's egregious-clickbait enforcement outside India, and whether strikes now apply, was not confirmed.
- YouTube has not disclosed how satisfaction is weighted against watch time, or how title/thumbnail-content mismatch is detected.
- No equivalent official TikTok or Instagram statement on misleading hooks was researched.
